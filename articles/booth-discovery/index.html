The booth is a discovery call with worse acoustics.

I did not stop people in their tracks by telling them what my software does. I stopped them with "How's Vegas treating ya?"

Oct 23, 2026 · 3 min read

Then I talked about anything other than the product until they asked. When they did, I gave them one line, high level: "Oh, we're analytics." Then I turned it back on them: "But what do you do, what brought you to Vegas this time around?"

That was the whole technique. And it worked, because a booth is not a pitch. It is a discovery call with worse acoustics.

A discovery call has a calendar invite, an agenda, thirty minutes booked. A booth has none of that. No invite, no agenda, no booked time. Just a person, a problem, and thirty seconds. If you cannot discover in thirty seconds on a loud floor, the calendar invite was never the thing doing the work. The question was.

Here is what four days on the floor taught me, in order.

1. The first sentence is the real problem.

"How's Vegas treating ya?" is not a pitch. It is a door. People walk through it with the real thing, because nobody opens up to a pitch, they open up to a person. They lead with the pain, then they dress it up with the polite version. The polite version is the second sentence. The first sentence is the one they actually came to say. Listen to that one. Everything after it is editing.

2. The product is the last thing, not the first.

If you lead with it, you are pitching to someone who has not decided to listen yet. So I did not lead with it. I talked about Vegas, about their trip, about anything except the product, until they asked. Then one line, high level: "Oh, we're analytics." Then the question back: "But what do you do?" The demo is the answer to a question they have not asked yet. Wait until they ask it. The demo lands ten times harder when it answers the sentence they just said.

3. The people who come back are the pipeline.

Day one is badge scans. Anyone can scan a badge. Day three is the person who came back because they thought about it overnight and have a real question. Count the return visits, not the scans. The scans are a vanity metric with a lanyard.

4. Discovery is a function of question quality, not meeting length.

Thirty minutes on a calendar does not make a discovery call. One sharp question does. The booth proved it, because there was no meeting to hide behind. No agenda, no deck, no "let me walk you through." Just the question, and whether it was good enough to make someone stop walking.

The booth did not teach me how to pitch. It taught me the pitch was never the job. The job was always the question, and the question works the same on a loud expo floor as it does on a quiet Zoom. On the floor, the question was "How's Vegas treating ya?" On a call, it is whatever version of that fits the room. The floor just makes it obvious, because there is nothing else to hide behind.

Discovery was never the meeting. It was always the question.
